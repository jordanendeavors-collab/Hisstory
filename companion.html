IA Note on the Two Bibles

Two Bibles sit open on the table. One is the family inheritance — a leather-bound King James edition printed in 1862, carrying its Reformation-era chronology in a small cartouche at the head of the chapter and its cross-references in narrow side columns. The other is a modern study Bible kept open beside it, set in clean type with paragraph-style commentary running below each block of verses. Between them, in a third place on the table, lies a spiral-bound notebook in which the current custodian copied the chapter day by day in the spring of 2026.

The 1862 is a King James Bible — descended in unbroken line from the translation William Tyndale began in 1525, Coverdale completed in 1535, and a committee of forty-seven scholars revised under James I in 1611. The text the custodian holds was set in type two and a half centuries after that committee finished its work, but the translation itself is unchanged. The English of the 1862 is the English of 1611 — its thees and thous, its firmament, its replenish the earth, its moveth upon the face of the waters. When the family's ancestors stepped off the Ark and Dove at St. Clement's Island in March of 1634, this was the English Bible they would have known.

The modern is, by every internal sign, a study edition of one of the twentieth-century formal-equivalence translations — its phrasing matches the New American Standard Bible's idiom closely (expanse where the 1862 has firmament, sea monsters where the 1862 has whales, govern where the 1862 has rule). Its running commentary, its writing-date tables, its chronology charts of patriarchs and kings, all mark it as a study Bible rather than a reading Bible — designed for an open notebook beside it, exactly as the custodian has used it.

What this companion document does is read them side by side, verse by verse, with the custodian's notebook entries on the same page where they apply. The 1862 text is the inheritance; the modern text is the apparatus that helps a present-day reader understand what the inheritance is saying; the notebook is the personal record that turns the act of reading into something the next generation can hold.

IIThe Calendar at the Chapter's Head

Before any verse, the 1862 carries a chapter cartouche the modern Bible has dropped. Six lines of calendrical reckoning sit beneath the chapter heading, naming the year of Christ, the Julian Period, the Cycle of the Sun, the Dominical Letter, the Cycle of the Moon, the Indiction, and the year from the Hebrew month of Tishri. Almost no English Bible printed after 1900 carries this block. To a twenty-first-century reader its lines look like a code; to a seventeenth-century reader it was the page that anchored the chapter in time itself.

The Year before the common Year of Christ 4004 follows Bishop James Ussher's chronology, published in 1650 and adopted into the Authorized Version as a marginal date scheme through the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. The Julian Period 0710 is a position in a seven-thousand-nine-hundred-and-eighty-year astronomical cycle invented by Joseph Scaliger in 1583 and used as a universal day-count for chronological calculation. The Cycle of the Sun 0010 is a twenty-eight-year solar repetition for determining which day of the week any date falls on. The Cycle of the Moon — the famous Metonic cycle of 19 years — governs the dating of Easter. The Indiction 0005 is a fifteen-year Roman fiscal cycle that survived inside Catholic and Orthodox ecclesiastical calendars long after the empire it served was gone. The Creation from Tisri 0001 is the Hebrew Anno Mundi reckoned from the month of Tishri, the first month of the Jewish civil year.

Each line gave the seventeenth-century reader a different way of locating themselves in time. The modern Bible has retired the whole block as an artifact, partly because Ussher's young-earth chronology is no longer credible to most readers and partly because none of the other cycles answer questions a present-day reader is asking. What's lost is the sense that this chapter sits inside time — that Creation, like every other event in the chronicle, can be plotted in years and cycles. The custodian's notebook copied the cartouche on its first page, line by line, as an act of preservation: even when the chronology itself is no longer believed, the format of the older reader's mind is worth keeping.

One small detail of the transcription is worth recording. The 1862's cartouche prints the Cycle of the Moon as 0007; the custodian's notebook records it as 0004. Whether the notebook misread the line in low light or whether the print is itself unclear is still an open question, and one of the items for the next reading session.

IIITwo Centuries of Translation Philosophy

Translation philosophy is the invisible architecture behind every Bible. It governs every choice the translators make, from the grandest theological term to the smallest preposition. The 1862 and the modern follow different philosophies, and reading them side by side is the easiest way to see how the choice plays out across a single chapter.

The King James tradition — and so the 1862 — practices what translators call formal equivalence. The principle is to follow the original Hebrew or Greek word by word and grammatical structure by grammatical structure, preserving the original's word order, idiom, and even its sentence-length wherever English can bear it. When the Hebrew uses the same word in two places, the English uses the same word in both. When the Hebrew has a participle rather than a finite verb, the English tries to render that. The result is a translation that reads as English but feels foreign in its bones — the rhythms are not native English rhythms. The 1862's And the earth was without form, and void; and darkness was upon the face of the deep is one of the loveliest sentences in English, but it isn't a sentence anyone would construct from scratch. It's English shaped to Hebrew.

The modern study Bible practices something between formal and dynamic equivalence, sometimes called functional equivalence. The principle is to translate the meaning of the original rather than its grammatical form — to produce a sentence that conveys what the Hebrew or Greek meant, in the natural English a present-day reader would use to convey the same meaning. The modern's The earth was formless and void is closer to natural English; formless is a single adjective doing the work of without form, and the sentence reads almost like a plain prose description.

Neither approach is wrong. Formal equivalence preserves more of the original's strangeness, which is theologically valuable — the Hebrew Bible is strange, and flattening its strangeness flattens its texture. Dynamic equivalence preserves more of the original's meaning, which is also valuable — a sentence no one can understand isn't really translated at all. Most serious readers use both kinds together, which is exactly what the custodian has done by keeping the 1862 and the modern open at the same time. The 1862 is closer to the Hebrew's shape; the modern is closer to the Hebrew's sense. Between the two, the chapter comes into focus.

The custodian's notebook, written in their own hand, follows a third philosophy: reduction to definition. Each verse becomes one or two verb-pairs — Light → Day, Darkness → Night, Waters → Seas. The chapter is treated as a recipe of definitions rather than a narrative. This isn't a translation philosophy in any formal sense, but it's an honest reading method: when the verses themselves consist largely of God naming things into existence, the notebook captures exactly that — the naming, the definition, the moment a thing acquires its name.

1862

Modern

Modern uses quotation marks, capitalized 'He,' and 'one day' for the day count. — "one day"

The Hebrew is more open: God names this day, and only the next verse begins to imply there will be a second. The 1862 collapses that opening into the closure of a numbered sequence.

VThe Custodian's Notebook as a Reading

The notebook is not a translation. It is something rarer: a record of one reader's intelligence at work on a single chapter, in the spring of 2026, with both Bibles open beside them. Read across all six days, the notebook reveals four distinct interpretive moves, each worth noting because each is a reading the chapter genuinely sustains.

The first move is reduction to verb-pair definitions. Every day in the notebook is condensed to a small lexicon of name-changes: Light → Day, Darkness → Night, Water → Seas, Land → Earth, Firamnt → Heaven. The notebook treats Creation as a series of naming acts, not unlike a child first learning the names of things. The reading is shrewd because much of Genesis 1 is a series of naming acts: God said, God called, God blessed. The reduction strips away the prose and leaves the naming.

The second move is the recipe metaphor. On Day 3 the notebook records the phrase It takes about twenty minutes or so. Always evenly, morning good at the end — so make the mix, then bake. The custodian has noticed something the prose hides: each day works by combination and waiting. God speaks, God names, God sees — and only then does the evening arrive. The mix happens first, the bake happens after. This is one of the chapter's structural patterns made visible by the notebook's compression.

The third move is the second-shift metaphor. On Day 4 the notebook describes the lesser light, the moon, as ruling second shift. The greater light is the day rule, the lesser light is the relief crew — wage-labor terms for a divine governance of time. The metaphor isn't whimsical; it captures what the verse actually says, that one light rules the day and the other rules the night through a literal division of labor. Beneath that phrase, the notebook also carries a more lyrical reading: This was the divide, of equal force against darkness. The same custodian, in the same notebook page, sees both the laboring moon and the heroic moon.

The fourth move is the pronoun reading. On Day 6, where the chapter's grammar shifts from them (the creatures) to us (the divine plural in let us make man), the notebook follows the shift and circles the word us. Then, on the next line, the custodian writes MALE FEMALE HE THEM → US — tracking how the chapter's pronouns move from a generic third-person to a first-person plural that now includes the human. The reading is theologically sharp: the moment the human enters the chapter is the moment the chapter's us becomes a we that the reader is part of. The Bible is no longer about creation; it is now about creation including us.

"DAY 6 · is asking Firamnt · LET US · because now has Heavn · MAKE MAN IN OUR IMAGE · STARS — watchers · AFTER OUR LIKNESS · SUN/MOON — light · GOD CREATED MAN IN HIS OWN IMAGE · IMAGE GOD CREATD HE HIM · MALE FEMALE HE THEM → US"

The notebook also carries a small theological grammar that emerges across the days. The stars are read as watchers — a word not in either Bible but resonant with later Jewish and Christian angelology (the watchers of the Book of Daniel). The sun and moon are read as light, plain and elemental. The firmament is read as the thing God asks: on Day 6, the notebook records that it is asking the firmament — God addressing the population of the firmament he has just made, the let us as a question to the things he has placed there. This is one possible reading of the divine plural that translations have debated for two thousand years: not the Trinity, not the angelic council, but a direct address to the firmament's inhabitants.

VIThe Apparatus of Each Era

Every Bible carries its own apparatus — the marginal notes, cross-references, headings, study aids, and editorial conventions that surround the text. The 1862 and the modern carry different apparatuses, and the difference tells us what each era expected its readers to need.

The 1862's apparatus is concise and external. Down the inner margin runs a column of chapter-and-verse cross-references (the ch. 5. 1. & 9. 6. kind of notation): for each verse, a short list of other verses in the Bible that echo it or qualify it. The references are presumed to be enough — the reader is expected to know how to look them up, to consult them in another part of the Bible, to weigh them against the verse at hand. There is no explanatory prose. Where the Hebrew or Greek differs from the English in some interesting way, a brief marginal note flags it with the abbreviation Heb. or Or. — Heb. without form, Or. firmament. The reader is trusted to integrate the information into their own reading.

The modern's apparatus is extensive and explanatory. Beneath each block of verses runs a study-note paragraph in smaller type. The notes do explanatory work the older marginal references did not: they discuss the meaning of phrases, the historical setting, the theological implications, the lexical alternatives. Where the 1862 would say only Or. firmament, the modern says Or firmament. This could possibly have been a canopy of water vapor which acted to make the earth like a hothouse — and then a paragraph more on the canopy theory. The reader is presumed to need help, and is given a great deal of it.

Neither is the right amount of apparatus. The 1862's terseness flatters a reader who is already inside the tradition and knows its references by reflex; it leaves a reader who is outside the tradition stranded. The modern's prolixity helps the outside reader and may overwhelm the inside reader. The custodian's notebook, in its own way, performs the role both apparatuses are aiming at: it captures what the reader actually thought as they read, which neither marginal references nor paragraph commentary can. A future custodian sitting with this Bible and this notebook will see the chapter through the reader they inherited it from.

VIIWhat This Chapter Says About Itself

Genesis 1 is, on its surface, the chapter of how the world began. It is also — and this is what the comparison across two centuries makes visible — a chapter about naming. God creates and then names. Light becomes Day because God calls it Day. Darkness becomes Night because God calls it Night. The dry land becomes Earth, the waters become Seas, the firmament becomes Heaven. The act of creation is inseparable from the act of naming.

The chapter ends with one creature whose name God does not assign here. Man is created in God's image but is not named Man by God in Genesis 1; the naming of Adam, and of every other living creature, is deferred to Genesis 2:19, where Adam himself names them. The chapter of beginnings places naming in God's mouth; the next chapter places it in the human's mouth. The transition is structural — Genesis 1 makes us a creature that names by being the creature God created in his own image, the image being the capacity for the naming that God demonstrates throughout the chapter.

This is why translation differences in Genesis 1 matter more than they might in other chapters. The chapter is about the words God used to call things into being. To translate the chapter is to choose which English words will stand in for those original namings. The 1862 chose firmament, the modern chose expanse; the 1862 chose whales, the modern chose sea monsters; the 1862 chose dominion, the modern chose rule. Each choice is a small naming — a translator's name for what God named. The custodian's notebook, in keeping FIRAMNT through all six days, makes one more naming on top of all the others: the custodian's name for the same thing.

A heritage project preserves a chain of namings. The 1862 prints the KJV translators' names for the things of Creation; the modern prints the twentieth-century translators' names; the notebook records the custodian's names. When this Bible passes to Sylas, and from Sylas to those who come after, the chain extends further. Each generation that reads this chapter adds a small naming. The chapter that is about naming becomes the chapter the family names itself by.

VIIIFor the Next Reading

Three threads remain open from this reading. The first is the seventh day, the rest that closes the account of Creation in Genesis 2:1-3. The notebook stops at the close of Day 6; the seventh day waits for its own session, and deserves it. The reading of the Sabbath in both Bibles — the 1862's God ended his work against the modern's God completed His work — is one of the chapter's hardest verses, theologically and grammatically, and the custodian's notebook will want time with it.

The second thread is the Cycle of the Moon. The 1862's cartouche prints it as 0007; the notebook recorded it as 0004. The next reading should re-photograph the title page in better light and confirm the print. Whichever proves correct, the discrepancy itself is now part of the project's record — preserved honestly, not quietly resolved.

The third thread is the family. The chapter of Creation is the chapter of beginnings, and beginnings is what a Family Register is for. If this Bible carries a register — and many 1862 family Bibles did — its pages record the family's own beginnings: births and marriages and deaths in the hands of those who held the book before. Photographing those pages and transcribing the entries verbatim is the next session that matters. The chapter of Creation gave God's namings; the Family Register gives ours. Both belong in this project.
